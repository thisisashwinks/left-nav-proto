Switchyard · left nav

Add product explorations

Six ways to let someone put a thing into an L2 panel without first making them work out whether the thing is a product or a sub-product, and a seventh surface that turns several rows into one parent. Every mock on this page is clickable. None of them is a spec.

The problem, stated honestly

The nav is a three-level tree and the data model knows it perfectly well. childOf records a child's parent, childrenFor resolves a row's children, and withChildFiled will refile any child under any product. Three rungs, cleanly stored, with no ambiguity anywhere in grouping.ts.

The vocabulary is the part nobody has. "L2" and "L3" are our words; "product" and "sub-product" are a translation of our words that is no better, because the catalogue cheerfully contradicts it — Workflows is a child of Automation and is also the single most used destination in the account, while Subscriptions is a top-rung product with no children at all and the depth of a bookmark. Someone looking at those two rows has no reason to believe they are different kinds of thing, because in every way they can perceive, they aren't. They are both one click to one screen.

So the current cascading picker asks the user to answer a question they cannot answer, and it asks it first: walk into a category, then a product, then maybe its children. The only way to use it correctly is to already know the shape of the tree you are about to add to. That is the confusion — not the chrome, not the chevrons. Rebuilding the same cascade with nicer rows will not move it.

The real tension. The tree is a fact about storage. Level is a fact about place. The picker fails because it makes the user supply the storage fact. Every approach below is some way of deriving it from a place instead — from the row you invoked, the row you point at, the indent you can see, or the thing you do immediately afterwards.
And one more thing we have not been saying out loud. In this codebase every add is a move. addProductToGroup runs withRowDetached then withProductFiled; moveChildToProduct runs withRowDetached then writes childOf. A row lives in exactly one place. So "add Workflows to Revenue" silently empties it out of Automation — and if it was Automation's last child, lostAllChildren fires and the save is blocked. The picker currently says nothing about any of this until after the fact. Whatever we pick, this has to be visible in the list, not in the toast.

Principles I'll judge these against

  1. Never ask for a level. If the user has to classify the thing before the system will show it to them, the design has failed, however pretty the cascade is.
  2. Show where it lands before it lands. Indent is visible; "L3" is not. Prefer the pixel to the word.
  3. One list for finding. People search by name. The tree is good for browsing and terrible for retrieval — pageOptions already exists precisely because somebody noticed this.
  4. Say that it moves. Every add is a move. The list should name the place it is taking the row out of, at the moment of choosing, not in the toast.
  5. The structural consequence should be reversible in one gesture. Making something a parent changes a page into a panel. That is a big edit to commit by accident.
  6. Buildable on the actions that exist. addProductToGroup, moveChildToProduct, moveChildToGroup, placeAtL1. If an approach needs a seventh verb, that is a cost and I should say so.

The mocks below all start from the same panel — the Revenue category, with Invoices & estimates and Payments already holding children — and the same slice of the real catalogue, so they can be compared on the same task: put Workflows and Estimates somewhere sensible.

Approach A

Flat search-first — the list has no levels in it

One field over every rung of the catalogue. The result carries its own breadcrumb; the destination was already decided by the + you pressed.

Hover a row for its + (adds inside that row), or a gap for its + (adds to the panel).
The picker. Try typing "work", "est" or "rep".
Nothing committed yet.

For

It removes the question entirely. There is no moment in this flow where the user is asked what kind of thing they are adding, because the answer was supplied by a gesture they made for a different reason — they pointed at a place. Retrieval is by name, which is how people actually remember destinations. And the breadcrumb under each result does quiet teaching: after a few adds you have absorbed that Workflows lives under Automation without ever having read the word "sub-product".

Against

The whole design rests on the user having pressed the right +, and those two +s are four pixels apart and both appear on hover. Press the wrong one and you get a correct-looking confirmation for the wrong structure, with nothing on screen contradicting you. It is also the least teachable option: the flow works by hiding the hierarchy, so a user who needs to reason about the hierarchy later — to fix a mistake, or to tidy a panel — has learnt nothing from it. And a flat list of ~90 entries with no grouping is genuinely bad for browsing; it only works if you already know a name.

Cost against the model

Lowest of the six. pageOptions already produces exactly this list, breadcrumb included, and productTreeOptionsDeep supplies the products; merge the two, sort, done. ADD_PRODUCT_SPOTS already gives seam-anchored invocation, and RowSeam's onAdd already passes the index. The only new work is a per-row + on the L2 row whose onPick routes to moveChildToProduct(id, rowId) instead of addProductToGroup. Roughly a day.
Approach B

Destination-first — level is a place, not a type

Pick where it goes, then pick what goes there. Step one lists the panel and every row in it, so "inside Payments" is an option you read rather than a level you deduce.

The panel stays put while you choose.
Two steps, with a real back control.
Nothing committed yet.

For

This is the only option where the structural decision is stated in words the user chose, which makes it the only one that is self-evidently recoverable: you know what you picked because you read it. It is also the most honest about what a panel is — a list of rows, some of which are containers — and it scales to the grouping ask without a new idea, because "inside Payments" and "inside a new group" are the same kind of option. Keyboard path falls out for free: one list, arrow keys, Enter.

Against

It is two steps where the old menu was one, and the first step is the one nobody wants to take — for the overwhelmingly common case ("just put it in this panel") you now pay a click to say so. Worse, it reintroduces the level question in disguise: "this panel" versus "inside Payments" is the L2/L3 distinction, written out. We have made it concrete, which helps, but a user who does not know whether Estimates belongs under Payments still does not know, and now we have given them a screen that insists they decide before they are allowed to see the list of things.

Cost against the model

Low. RowMenuAction.options plus onPick already does two-level menus with a back control, which is what row-menu.tsx's "second view rather than a submenu" note describes. Step one is category.productIds mapped to options; step two is the merged flat list from A. Dispatch is a two-way branch on the step-one choice: addProductToGroup or moveChildToProduct. One to two days.
Approach C

Landing preview — show the indent instead of naming it part of the recommendation

One search list, and beside it the panel as it will be, with a ghost row sitting at the indent it will occupy. You move the ghost with ↑ ↓ and Tab / ⇧Tab, or by clicking the preview. Nothing is named "level" anywhere.

Pick something on the left, then nudge the ghost on the right. Tab indents, ⇧Tab outdents, Enter commits.
Nothing committed yet.

For

It is the only approach that makes the consequence legible before commit, which is the actual complaint. "L3" is a word with no referent; a row sitting 22px to the right under Payments is a picture of exactly what will be true afterwards. It also lets the structural edge cases show themselves rather than be explained: indent under a childless row and the preview draws the disclosure triangle appearing, so "this turns Subscriptions into a panel" is something you watched happen, not a warning you dismissed. And the ghost gives us a natural home for the move warning — it can render the row fading out of its old parent in the same preview.

Against

It is the most expensive thing here and it buys the least for the easy case: someone adding a plain product to a plain panel now has a second pane they have to look at and ignore. The preview is also a promise we may not be able to keep — the real panel scrolls, has ~40 rows in a busy account, and sits at 360px; a faithful miniature of it inside a 560px popover is either unreadably small or a lie about what the panel looks like. And it quietly makes the popover a modal surface, which breaks the "menu over the row you are editing" pattern the rest of edit mode uses. There is a real risk we ship a diagram rather than a control.

Cost against the model

Highest. Needs a pure projection — given NavLayoutState, a row id and a target position, what does childrenFor/resolveGroups return? — computed without committing. That is tractable because every verb in grouping.ts is already a pure state → state function: run withChildFiled on a copy and render the result. So the preview is honest by construction, which is the nice part. The expensive part is the UI: a second pane, keyboard handling for indent, and drag/click targets inside a miniature. Three to five days.
Approach D

Outliner inline — type into the list, Tab to nest

No popover at all. The panel grows an empty row, you type, autocomplete offers matches from the catalogue, and Tab tucks the row under the one above it. The Notion / Workflowy model, applied to a nav.

Click Add item, type "work" or "est", pick a match, then press Tab before Enter.
 
The indent indicator sits on the row itself. Nothing in this flow has a name for what you are doing — you are just typing in a list and pushing a row to the right.
Nothing committed yet.

For

It has the shortest distance between intent and result of anything here, and it borrows a mental model that a large fraction of our users already carry in from Notion, Linear and every outliner since 1984. There is no picker to design, no destination step, no separate preview — the thing you are editing is the thing you are looking at, so of course it lands where you can see it landing. It also makes the grouping ask nearly free, because indenting an existing row is the same gesture as indenting a new one.

Against

Tab is invisible. Discoverability for a keyboard-only affordance in a mouse-driven settings panel is poor, and the usual fix — a chevron or a drag-right handle — reintroduces an affordance that must be explained. More seriously, this flow implies you can type a new thing, and you cannot: the nav only holds catalogue rows, so a free-text field that rejects most of what you type is a cruel input. We would be pretending the panel is a document when it is a constrained picker wearing a document's clothes. And the whole thing is an unusual pattern for an admin settings surface, so it carries real training cost for agencies with low-tech staff.

Cost against the model

Medium, and in an awkward place. The store is fine — indent is moveChildToProduct(id, rowAboveId), outdent is moveChildToGroup(id, category.id, index), both of which exist. The cost is in flyout-panel.tsx: a row that is an input, focus and caret management inside a floating panel that already closes on outside click, and an autocomplete popover anchored inside another popover. Three days, most of it fighting the flyout's own dismissal behaviour.
Approach E

Add then place — land flat, nest as a second thought part of the recommendation

Every add goes to the panel. Then the row itself offers, once, to tuck under a neighbour — and offers to undo. The level question is never asked; it is offered, after the user can see the result.

Add anything. Watch what appears under the new row.
A single flat list. No destination step at all.
Nothing committed yet.

For

It is the only option that asks the question at the one moment the user is equipped to answer it: after the row exists and they can see it sitting in the list. Before that moment, "should this be under Payments?" is abstract; afterwards it is a thing you look at. It also makes the common case genuinely one click, degrades to correct rather than to wrong when ignored (a flat row is always a legitimate nav row; a wrongly-nested one is not), and it gives us an obvious home for the move warning and the undo.

Against

It is a nag. A strip that appears after every add, 90% of which will be dismissed, is a tax on the common path to serve the uncommon one — and the usual consequence is that people learn to swat it without reading, at which point it is costing clicks and teaching nothing. It also lands the row at the wrong level first for anyone who knew what they wanted, which is a small insult to the competent user, and it means two state commits for one intent, so the undo stack now has a step in it that does not correspond to anything the user thinks they did. If a lot of adds are genuinely sub-items, this is the worst option here.

Cost against the model

Low, and it composes with anything. addProductToGroup then optionally moveChildToProduct — both exist, both already commit with a message, and commit() already carries undo. The only new piece is a transient "just added" id on the panel and a row-level strip. One day. Worth noting it is the only approach on this page that can be shipped on top of another one rather than instead of it.
Approach F

Multi-select — the panel is a selection surface

Check several things in the picker and add them in one commit; check several rows in the panel and group them. One selection idea, two jobs — which is why this is also the answer to the second ask.

Check rows in either place. The action bar changes to match.
Checkboxes in the picker too; add them all at once.
Nothing committed yet.

For

Multi-select is the one interaction on this page that users have already met everywhere — file managers, inboxes, every table in our own product. It makes bulk setup (the real job, when an agency is configuring a template) dramatically cheaper, and it solves grouping without inventing a vocabulary: you do not nest a row, you select three rows and give them a name, and the nesting is a consequence you can see. It also turns the scariest structural edit into the most explicit one, since you had to tick three boxes to get there.

Against

It answers the grouping ask well and the original ask badly. Multi-select tells you nothing about where the checked things land — four boxes ticked and one "Add 4 items" button is maximally silent about structure, so on its own it is the current menu with more steps. It also needs a mode: checkboxes always on make a settings panel feel like a bulk-admin table, and checkboxes behind a "Select" toggle are a mode users forget they are in. And "group these" raises a naming question (uniqueGroupLabel will hand you "New group 2") at the exact moment the user is least prepared to name anything.

Cost against the model

Medium, and it is the one place the model needs something new. Adding n products is n calls to addProductToGroup, which means n entries in the undo stack and n toasts — so this wants a batched commit, which nav-layout-provider.tsx hints at already ("one method rather than a loop of moveProductToGroup calls"). Grouping needs a new verb that creates a parent and refiles the selection; see the next section for what that verb is made of. Two to four days depending on whether batched undo lands with it.
The second ask

Grouping several L2 rows into one parent

The model already supports the end state and has no verb for the transition. withChildFiled(state, childId, parentId) refiles a child under a product, and childrenFor resolves children for a product or a promoted child — so a parent that owns the grouped rows is an expressible state. What does not exist is "make this row the parent", because today every parent is a catalogue fact.

The design question underneath it. What is the new parent? Three answers, and they are not the same product.
1 · A new empty row — a label and an icon that owns the selection and opens a panel. Needs a new kind of nav entity (a "folder product"), because productById will never answer for it.
2 · One of the selected rows, promoted — group Invoices, Payments and Subscriptions "under Payments". Expressible today: withChildFiled twice. But it means picking a winner, and the winner's own page is now also a panel.
3 · A category — which is what withChildInGroup and customGroups already are, one rung up. Cheapest of all, and arguably what people actually mean by "group these" — except it makes an L1, not an L2 parent, which is not what was asked.
The mock below does 1, because that is the ask as stated. It is also the most expensive answer, and I want that visible rather than smuggled in.
Click Select, tick two or three rows, then Group items. Expand the result and use its kebab to ungroup.
 
The naming step defaults to a joined name — "Billing" from Invoices and Payments is a guess, not a rule; the field is focused and selected so the first keystroke replaces it.
Nothing committed yet.

The flow, step by step

  • Entry point. A Select control in the panel's title row, beside Add product — not a kebab item. Grouping is a thing you do to several rows, so the control cannot live on one row's menu. A row's own kebab gains "Group with…" as a shortcut that enters select mode with that row already ticked, so the one-row entry point exists too.
  • Selection affordance. Checkboxes replace the drag grips while the mode is on, and the whole row is a hit target. The mode is loud on purpose: the action bar is pinned to the top of the panel and says how many are selected, because a quiet mode that reorders rows on click is how someone destroys a nav by accident.
  • Naming. Inline, in the panel, at the position the group will occupy — not a modal. The field is pre-filled with a guess and fully selected. groupNameTaken already exists and should be wired to it, with the same fold-case rule, so "payments" cannot be created beside "Payments". Esc abandons and restores the selection rather than the whole panel.
  • Afterwards. The group lands at the position of the topmost selected row, expanded, with the grouped rows inside in their previous relative order. The panel does not re-sort. One toast, one undo entry, naming the group and the count.
  • Grouping a row that already has children. The case the mock surfaced and I had not thought about: select Invoices & estimates — which owns All invoices and Recurring invoices — and those two would land at a fourth rung the nav does not draw. There is no good silent answer. Flattening them up beside their old parent is what the mock does and what the warning in the naming step says; the alternative is to refuse the selection, which is cleaner to reason about and worse to use, because the row you most want to group is usually the one with things in it. I would ship the flatten with the warning, and I would want this specific decision reviewed, because it is the only place in the flow where a user can lose a structure they built.
  • Ungroup. On the group row's kebab, as a first-class verb — not "delete". It spills the children back at the group's own position in their current order and removes the parent. The one thing it must never do is take the children with it, which is exactly the trap lostAllChildren is watching for one rung up.
What this costs the data model, honestly. A synthetic parent is a row with an id that neither productById nor childById can resolve, and this codebase leans on exactly that resolution in a dozen places — labelForProduct, iconForProduct, glyphFor, isRowHiddenDeep, childrenFor, lostAllChildren. Either every one of those grows a third fallback, or NavLayoutState gains a syntheticRows: Record<string, {label, iconName, childIds}> and a resolver that the existing ones delegate to. The second is right and it is a week, not a day. lostAllChildren is the sharp edge: ungrouping leaves a synthetic row with zero children, which that function will flag and block the save on — so ungroup has to delete the parent in the same commit, not in two.

Edge-case matrix

Behaviour per approach where they differ, and the recommendation's answer where that is what matters. "R" = what I'd ship (C + A + E, see below).

Case A · Flat search B · Destination-first C · Landing preview E · Add then place F · Multi-select
Item already lives elsewhere in the nav — every add is a move (withProductFiled, withRowDetached) R: result row carries a right-aligned "from Automation" in the list itself, before the click. Toast reads "Moved Workflows from Automation to Revenue", as addProductToGroup's message already does. Same hint, but it arrives one step later — the user has already committed to a destination before they learn the item has a current home. Strongest: the preview can render the row leaving its old parent. Costs a second miniature, so more likely we settle for the hint. Weakest at the moment of choosing, strongest afterwards — the nudge strip is already there and can carry "This moved out of Automation. Undo". Dangerous: four ticked rows can be four silent moves out of four different parents. Needs a pre-commit summary ("3 items will move out of Automation, Reporting").
Adding a sub-item to a row with no children — the row stops being a page and becomes a panel Happens silently. Needs an explicit confirm line in the picker header: "Subscriptions opens a page today. Adding an item here turns it into a menu." Step one can mark it: "Inside Subscriptions · becomes a menu". R: the preview draws the disclosure chevron appearing on Subscriptions. Shown, not said — this is the case C exists for. Can't happen on the add; happens on the nudge, where the dropdown can mark which targets would change shape. Only reachable via grouping, where it is the entire point and therefore fine.
Adding into a row that is itself a promoted L3 (childById answers; it can own children) R: allowed, with no special casing in the UI — childrenFor already falls through to childById for exactly this, and the comment there says why. The one guard that must hold is withChildFiled's refusal of a move that makes a row its own ancestor; the picker must therefore exclude the target row and its whole subtree from the list, which exclude() does not currently do recursively. This is a real bug waiting in the deep picker today.
Moving the last child out of a row — lostAllChildren fires, emptiedParents blocks the save R: warn in the list, at the moment of choosing, on the row that would cause it: "Last item in Automation". Do not block the pick — blocking a legal edit because of a consequence two steps away is how the current save-blocked state feels arbitrary. Commit it, and let the existing emptied-parent warning carry the resolution, which already offers the right fix. The mocks in A and C show this hint live.
Search matches at two levels at once, or a parent whose child also matches R: show both, never collapse, and never dedupe to the parent. "Reporting" matches the product and nine of its children; the answer is one result for the product and nine for the children, each with its own breadcrumb, with exact-prefix matches on the name ranked above matches on the path. Highlight the matched substring so a path-only match is visibly a path-only match. The one thing to avoid is the clever version where matching a parent hides its children — that is how someone concludes Estimates does not exist.
Not entitled / not provisioned (addableProducts excludes it) R: out of the list entirely, as today. addableProducts is deliberately wider than enabledProducts so a row removed a moment ago can come back, and that is the right rule — but a product the agency never sold should not be a greyed row advertising an upsell inside a nav editor. If product wants the upsell, it belongs in a separate "More products" section at the bottom of the list, visually separated, never interleaved with things that will actually work.
Long labels Single line, middle of the row ellipsised, breadcrumb truncated from the left (… › Automation › Workflows) because the last segment is the one that identifies. Native title tooltip. "Documents & contracts" and "Invoices & estimates" are the real worst cases and both fit at 320px; the real risk is renamed rows, where labelForProduct can produce "Opportunities › Settings" and then the picker adds a path on top of a qualifier. In that case the qualifier wins and the path is dropped.
40+ products, deep catalogue Flat list is virtualised or capped at ~50 with "keep typing to narrow". Browsing a 90-row flat list is bad, which is why R keeps the grouped-by-category rendering when the field is empty and switches to a flat ranked list the moment a character is typed. One surface, two states — not two menus.
Empty category, empty panel The dashed Add product row is the whole panel, centred, with one line of copy. ADD_PRODUCT_STYLES's dashed variant already draws "the outline of a row that does not exist yet", which is exactly right here. No illustration — this is a menu, not a page.
Keyboard R: the add control takes focus on open and the field is live immediately, so typing works before anything is clicked. ↑↓ move the highlight, Enter commits at the shown position, Esc closes with nothing committed. In C, Tab/⇧Tab move the ghost's indent and do not move focus while the picker is open — which is a real accessibility cost and needs an explicit visible hint, or an alternative (Alt+→) for anyone who cannot give up Tab.
Undo R: one add is one commit() and therefore one undo entry, which already works. The two cases that break it: E's nudge (two commits for one intent — must be coalesced, or the nudge's own Undo must undo both), and F's multi-add (n commits — needs the batched verb). The group/ungroup pair must each be a single commit for the reason in the warning above.

Recommendation

Ship A's list inside C's frame, with E as the safety net. Ship F only for grouping.

Concretely: one surface, invoked from the seam or the dashed add row. It opens with the catalogue grouped by category — browsing shape — and flattens into a ranked, breadcrumbed, path-highlighted list the moment a character is typed. Beside the list is the panel-as-it-will-be, with a ghost row at the position the current selection would take; Tab and clicks in the preview move that ghost's indent. Commit is one action. If the user ignores the preview entirely they get a flat add, which is always a correct outcome, and E's strip gives them a one-click way to nest it afterwards without reopening anything.

The reasoning is that A alone solves finding and nothing else — it is the right list and it is silent about structure, which is half of the complaint. C alone solves structure and nothing else — it is the right feedback attached to a browse that is slow. Together they cover the two halves without either one having to ask the user what level means. B and D are both coherent and I'd be happy to be argued into D, but B spends its first step on the question we are trying to stop asking, and D's Tab is invisible in a surface nobody will read instructions for.

The single strongest objection to this

The preview may be a diagram rather than a control. A 220px miniature of a 360px panel that in a busy account holds 40 rows and scrolls cannot both be faithful and be readable. The moment it stops being faithful it is worse than nothing, because it teaches a shape that does not match what the user sees when they close the picker — and the whole justification for the expense was faithfulness. If the miniature has to scroll independently, or truncate, or omit the rows above and below the insertion point, then we have paid three to five days for a picture, and A plus E alone would have done the same work for two. That is the thing to settle before this becomes a spec, and it is settled by trying C against the overloaded nav volume, not against this page's tidy four-row Revenue panel.

What I'd test, and what each test settles

Each of these resolves one question, not "which do you prefer". Same task every time: put Workflows into Revenue, under Payments — then undo it, which is the half nobody tests.

A · Flat search-first
Does the invoking + actually carry the destination in people's heads, or do they open the picker and then start looking for where to put it? If they look for a destination control inside the picker, A's whole premise is wrong and B is right.
B · Destination-first
Does naming the destination in prose ("inside Payments") let people answer a question they could not answer as "L2 or L3"? If yes, the problem was vocabulary and B is cheap and sufficient.
C · Landing preview
Do people look at the preview at all, and does it survive a 40-row panel? Instrument hover and eye-line in the busy case, not the tidy one. If they never look, the recommendation collapses to A + E.
D · Outliner
Does anyone discover Tab unprompted? And when told, do they then expect to be able to type a row that is not in the catalogue? The second finding matters more than the first.
E · Add then place
What fraction of adds genuinely want nesting? If it is under ~15%, the nudge is a nag and should be demoted to a row-kebab item. If it is over ~40%, E is the wrong default and the level decision belongs up front.
F · Multi-select + group
When someone says "group these", do they mean a new parent row, a category, or just "put them next to each other"? That is the question that decides whether grouping costs a day or a week — and it is answerable with a paper exercise before any of this is built.

Before this becomes a spec

  1. Is the new parent a real row or a folder? The grouping section's three answers are three different products. Answer 2 — promote one of the selection — ships this week on verbs that exist. Answer 1 needs syntheticRows in NavLayoutState and a resolver every label/icon/visibility function delegates to. I have mocked 1; I would ship 2 first if the deadline is real.
  2. Is "add is a move" a decision we still stand behind, or an artefact? The Aug 21 note says duplication was ruled out for MVP because it made breadcrumbs ambiguous. That reasoning is sound for breadcrumbs and much weaker for a nav where the same destination legitimately belongs in two places. If duplication is back on the table, half of this edge-case matrix evaporates and A alone is probably enough.
  3. Who is this for? Everything above assumes the agency admin configuring a template once, not the sub-account user. permissionsFor says only agency can restructure. If the real user is an agency admin doing this in a setup session, bulk (F) matters far more than per-add elegance and the recommendation should probably be reordered.

Exploration only. Nothing here is a commitment, and no file under src/ was touched.
